The import

I ran both imports myself. I arranged the purchases with an exporter in Japan, who handled the Japan side through to loading the trucks onto the ship. From there I took over: I engaged customs agents in British Columbia, prepared the CBSA documentation, completed the federal import exemptions, and arranged inland transport to Winnipeg and delivery to my home. I then restored the trucks, marketed them, sold the Hijet and kept the Carry as my shop truck.

Electrical

Suzuki Carry: daytime running lights on a ground-switched headlight

Problem. The Carry's H4 headlights are switched on the ground side, while standard daytime running light modules output +12 V. Off-the-shelf kits do not work on this system without cutting factory wiring.

Solution. A two-relay harness that mounts entirely at the headlight corner. Relay 1 converts the module's +12 V signal into a ground path for the low beam. Relay 2 watches the high-beam wire and tells the module to drop the running lights, so two filaments are not lit at once.

Result. Three tap points only (the wiper circuit, the H4 plug and a chassis ground), joined with Posi-Tap connectors. The harness does not run to the battery or cut any factory wire, and it unplugs to return the truck to stock.

Daihatsu Hijet: a four-relay lighting circuit

Problem. The Hijet's factory combination switch was faulty: selecting high beam blew the headlight fuse, and the truck did not have daytime running lights.

Solution. Four relays, each with one job: a running-light relay, a five-pin selector relay that switches between running lights and low beam, a tail and park relay, and an "OR" relay that holds the selector during high beam. The circuit has its own 12 AWG battery feed with a 15 A fuse within three inches of the positive terminal.

Result. Running lights, low and high beams all work correctly. The wiring follows the factory harness, passes through factory grommets, is grounded on a factory bolt sealed with dielectric grease, and is hidden behind the original trim.

Keystone Japan Import Co.

After importing both trucks, I designed a proof-of-concept business around the process: an agent that imports Japanese auction vehicles, machinery and parts to order for Winnipeg customers. I wrote the full business plan and built the website from the ground up. It remains a proof of concept and was never started.

The business plan

  • Written before any money or customer was committed, after my own two imports showed that holding inventory was the wrong model.
  • An agent model rather than a dealer model: the customer is the importer of record and pays a deposit and sets a written maximum bid before anything is bought, so the business does not own any vehicles.
  • A risk table with a mitigation for each risk: currency, shipping delays, regulatory change, supplier concentration, demand and the key person.
  • A ten-step pipeline from auction to delivery, with the unit economics, market position, partner arrangements and risks set out.
  • Fleet pricing for customers who order three or more trucks in one container.

The website

  • Designed and built from scratch, with an English and Japanese language toggle.
  • Separate pages for kei trucks, tractors and machinery, cars and parts, plus an auction guide, pricing and a step-by-step explanation of the process.
  • A quote request flow and a fleet pricing offer.
